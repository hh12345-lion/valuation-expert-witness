Valuing a loss-making or early-stage business can present particular challenges in litigation. A company may have limited trading history, negative earnings, incomplete financial information, or forecasts that depend on future investment and development. These circumstances can make some conventional valuation approaches more difficult to apply without careful consideration of the available evidence.

A valuation expert may therefore need to consider the nature of the business, its financial position, available market evidence and the assumptions supporting any proposed valuation. The appropriate approach will depend on the circumstances of the case, the relevant valuation date and the questions within the scope of the expert's instruction.

## Why Loss-Making Businesses Can Be Difficult to Value

Many established business valuation approaches make use of historical financial performance, earnings or cash flow. A business that is consistently loss-making may provide less historical evidence from which to assess future maintainable performance.

An early-stage company can present different issues. It may have limited trading history because it is still developing its products, entering a market or building its customer base.

The absence of established profitability does not, by itself, determine the value attributed to a business. The available evidence may instead need to be considered in the context of the company's circumstances at the relevant valuation date.

Relevant considerations may include:

- Trading history
- Revenue and cost trends
- Available cash and funding
- Existing contracts or customer relationships
- The stage of product or service development
- The commercial environment in which the business operates
- Management forecasts
- Previous investment or financing transactions
- Comparable businesses or transactions, where sufficiently relevant evidence exists

The significance of each factor will depend on the facts of the particular case.

## Establishing the Financial Position at the Valuation Date

The valuation date is an important part of the valuation exercise. The expert may need to consider information relevant to that date and the basis on which the valuation question has been framed.

For a loss-making company, this can involve examining more than the reported profit and loss account.

### Revenue and Cost Trends

Historical revenue may provide information about the development of the business, but a short trading history can make trends difficult to interpret.

An expert may examine:

- Revenue by period
- Recurring and non-recurring income
- Customer concentration
- Gross margins
- Operating expenditure
- Capital expenditure
- Changes in staffing or other significant costs

Historical information does not necessarily establish how the business will perform in the future. It can, however, form part of the evidence used to understand the business at the relevant valuation date.

### Cash and Funding Requirements

Cash resources can be particularly relevant for an early-stage business.

The analysis may consider available cash, existing borrowing, anticipated expenditure and identified funding requirements. Where further funding appears necessary for the business to continue or develop, the evidence surrounding that requirement may also be relevant to the valuation analysis.

## Considering Business Forecasts

Forecasts can be important when a company has not yet reached sustained profitability. They may provide information about anticipated revenue, costs, investment requirements and the expected development of the business.

A forecast is an assumption about future performance rather than a record of what has already occurred. Its relevance therefore depends on the evidence supporting the assumptions and the circumstances existing at the valuation date.

### Examining the Basis of a Forecast

A valuation expert may consider how a forecast was prepared and the evidence supporting its assumptions.

This can include questions such as:

- What assumptions underlie projected revenue?
- How were expected customer numbers determined?
- What evidence supports anticipated pricing?
- What costs are expected as the business expands?
- Is additional investment required?
- How does the forecast compare with actual performance before or after the valuation date, where such information is relevant and appropriate?

The valuation analysis may therefore consider the evidence supporting management projections rather than treating those projections as established outcomes.

### Actual Performance and Forecast Performance

Where later trading information is available, it may sometimes provide context when considering earlier forecasts. Its relevance will depend on the circumstances, the valuation date and the instructions given to the expert.

A later outcome does not necessarily establish what could reasonably have been known at an earlier date. This distinction can be important when considering valuation evidence in litigation.

## Possible Valuation Approaches

There is no single valuation method that necessarily applies to every loss-making or early-stage business. The appropriate approach depends on factors including the nature of the business, the available evidence and the purpose of the valuation.

### Income-Based Approaches

An income-based approach may be more difficult to apply where historical earnings are negative or there is insufficient evidence of sustainable future earnings.

Where future cash flows or earnings are used, the assumptions supporting those figures may require particular attention. These may include projected growth, margins, investment requirements and the period over which the business is expected to develop.

The suitability of an income-based approach will depend on the evidence available and the circumstances of the valuation.

### Market-Based Evidence

Comparable companies or transactions may sometimes provide useful evidence.

However, businesses that appear similar may differ in important respects, including size, sector, development stage, geography, revenue model and profitability. Those differences may affect the relevance of the comparison.

A valuation analysis may therefore consider both the similarities and differences between the subject business and available market evidence.

### Asset-Based Approaches

An asset-based approach may have relevance in some circumstances, particularly where the underlying assets provide meaningful evidence of value.

For an early-stage business, however, the value of its assets may not fully address questions concerning future commercial prospects, intellectual property or other characteristics of the business. The suitability of an asset-based approach therefore depends on the particular business and valuation question.

## Valuing Businesses With Negative Earnings

Negative earnings do not, by themselves, determine the value of a business.

Losses may arise for different reasons, including investment in development, expansion costs or continuing commercial difficulties. Distinguishing between these circumstances may require consideration of the available evidence.

An expert may consider whether losses appear connected with identifiable development activities, whether there is evidence supporting potential changes in future performance, and what resources may be required for that development.

These considerations should be assessed in the context of the valuation date rather than by assuming retrospectively that a particular outcome was inevitable.

## The Importance of Valuation Assumptions

Valuations of early-stage companies can be sensitive to assumptions about future performance.

Examples may include:

- Revenue growth
- Profit margins
- Customer retention
- Market expansion
- Capital requirements
- Operating costs
- Financing requirements
- Timing of future profitability

Changes in material assumptions, such as projected growth, margins or funding requirements, may affect the resulting valuation analysis. Clearly identifying material assumptions can therefore help explain how the valuation has been developed.

### Separating Evidence From Assumptions

A valuation report may distinguish between information supported by historical evidence and assumptions concerning future performance.

This can help the parties and the court understand which aspects of the valuation are based on observed information and which depend on estimates or projections.

## Valuation Evidence and Litigation

In litigation, the expert's analysis should address the questions within the scope of the instruction and the evidence available for consideration.

For a loss-making or early-stage company, this may involve reviewing financial records, business plans, forecasts, funding information, market evidence and other relevant material.

The expert may also need to identify limitations in the available evidence. Where information is incomplete or uncertain, explaining how that affects the valuation analysis can provide useful context rather than presenting an apparently precise figure without appropriate qualification.

## Questions Solicitors May Consider When Reviewing a Valuation

When a loss-making or early-stage business is the subject of valuation evidence, useful questions may include:

### What Was Known at the Valuation Date?

The relevant evidence should be considered in the context of the valuation date and the information appropriately available for the valuation exercise.

### How Reliable Are the Financial Forecasts?

Forecast assumptions may require careful examination, particularly where there is limited historical evidence against which to compare projected performance.

### Is There Relevant Market Evidence?

Comparable businesses and transactions may provide useful evidence, but their relevance depends on the degree of similarity and the quality of the available information.

### Are Material Valuation Assumptions Clearly Identified?

A clear distinction between established financial information, market evidence and forward-looking assumptions can help explain how the valuation analysis has been developed.

## Conclusion

Valuing a loss-making or early-stage business in litigation can involve issues that may be less prominent when valuing an established profitable company. Limited trading history, negative earnings, uncertain forecasts and funding requirements can affect the evidence available for analysis.

A valuation expert may therefore consider the company's financial position, development stage, forecasts, market evidence and relevant valuation assumptions in the context of the specific valuation date and instructions.

The appropriate valuation approach will depend on the circumstances of the business and the evidence available. Where uncertainty exists, identifying and explaining relevant assumptions and limitations can help present the valuation evidence clearly.
