Customer concentration can be an important consideration when valuing a business for litigation. A company may derive a substantial proportion of its revenue from one customer or a small number of customers. Depending on the circumstances, this reliance may affect the assessment of revenue sustainability, commercial risk and future business performance.

For solicitors and counsel involved in business valuation disputes, customer concentration can raise questions about the quality of earnings, the durability of customer relationships and the assumptions used in a valuation. The significance of these factors will depend on the available evidence, the nature of the business and the relevant valuation date.

A valuation expert may need to examine the extent of customer concentration, the contractual and commercial arrangements involved, and the potential implications for the valuation analysis. Customer concentration should not automatically be treated as evidence of reduced value. Its relevance depends on the specific circumstances.

## What Is Customer Concentration?

Customer concentration describes the extent to which a business depends on a limited number of customers for its revenue, earnings or commercial activity.

For example, a company might generate a substantial proportion of its annual revenue from a single customer. Another business might rely on a small group of customers for most of its sales. Both situations may warrant consideration during a business valuation, although the underlying risks may differ.

Customer concentration can occur across various sectors, including manufacturing, professional services, technology, distribution and specialist contracting. It may reflect the nature of the business model rather than an inherent weakness.

A business supplying specialist products under long-term contracts may have a different risk profile from one relying on informal, short-term customer relationships. The number of customers alone may therefore provide an incomplete picture.

## Why Customer Concentration May Matter in Business Valuation

Customer concentration may be relevant because revenue from a small customer base can make a business more exposed to changes in individual customer relationships.

However, the potential effect on value depends on factors such as the stability of demand, the duration of customer relationships, contractual protections and the availability of alternative customers.

### Revenue Sustainability

A valuation may involve an assessment of whether historical revenue provides a reasonable basis for considering future performance.

Where a significant proportion of revenue comes from one customer, an expert may examine the factors supporting the continuation of that income. These could include contract duration, renewal provisions, purchasing patterns and the history of the commercial relationship.

Historical revenue alone does not establish that the same level of income will continue. Equally, a concentrated customer base does not necessarily mean that revenue is unsustainable.

The relevant question is what the available evidence indicates about the business at the valuation date.

### Dependence on Individual Customers

The loss of a major customer may have different implications depending on how the business operates.

For example, a company might have the capacity to replace a departing customer relatively quickly. Another company may rely on a customer whose requirements are highly specialised and difficult to replace.

A valuation expert may consider the extent to which the business depends on particular customer relationships and whether there is evidence of realistic alternatives. The assessment may also need to distinguish between an identified risk and an assumption that a customer relationship will end.

### Profitability and Commercial Terms

A major customer may account for a substantial share of revenue without contributing a proportionate share of profit.

Pricing arrangements, discounts, service obligations, production costs and other contractual terms may affect the profitability of the relationship.

Where relevant information is available, an expert may examine the contribution of major customers to the business's overall financial performance. This can help distinguish revenue concentration from dependence on a particular customer for earnings.

## Evidence Used to Assess Customer Concentration

The assessment of customer concentration will depend on the available financial and commercial information. Relevant evidence may include accounting records, customer contracts, management reports and correspondence concerning significant commercial relationships.

### Customer Revenue Analysis

Customer-level revenue information may help establish the proportion of sales attributable to individual customers over a relevant period.

An expert may examine:

- Revenue generated by the largest customers
- Changes in customer rankings over time
- The proportion of total revenue attributable to major accounts
- The duration and frequency of customer orders
- Whether revenue is recurring, project-based or irregular
- The extent to which revenue depends on a single contract or purchasing decision

The period selected for analysis may matter. A customer responsible for a large share of revenue in one year may not represent the same level of dependence over a longer period.

The analysis should therefore be considered in context rather than relying on a single percentage without examining the underlying trading history.

### Customer Contracts and Renewal Terms

Contractual arrangements may provide information about the duration and conditions of a customer relationship.

Relevant terms may include contract expiry dates, renewal provisions, termination rights, minimum purchasing commitments and pricing arrangements. The practical significance of these provisions will depend on the wording of the contract and the surrounding commercial circumstances.

A long-term contract may provide some evidence of revenue visibility, but it does not necessarily eliminate commercial risk. Similarly, the absence of a long-term contract does not automatically establish that a customer relationship is likely to end.

The valuation analysis may need to consider both contractual protections and the broader evidence about how the relationship operates in practice.

### Customer Retention and Trading History

The history of a customer relationship may help explain whether revenue has been stable, growing, declining or irregular.

An expert may consider repeat orders, changes in purchasing volumes, customer retention patterns and any significant interruptions to trading. Where a business has experienced changes in its customer base, the reasons for those changes may also be relevant.

Past retention is informative but does not guarantee future retention. Its significance depends on the circumstances of the business and the nature of its customer relationships.

## Assessing Key Commercial Dependencies

Customer concentration may be connected with wider dependencies within a business. A major customer may also be the source of specialist technical requirements, access to a particular market or demand for a substantial part of the company's production capacity.

These relationships can require consideration beyond the proportion of revenue attributable to a customer.

### Switching Costs and Alternative Customers

The ease with which a business can replace a major customer may affect the assessment of commercial risk.

Relevant considerations may include the time needed to identify replacement customers, the cost of adapting products or services, regulatory or technical requirements and the availability of alternative demand.

Where the business operates in a specialised market, finding replacement customers may require a different period or level of investment than in a market with a broader customer base.

These factors should be assessed using evidence appropriate to the particular business. General assumptions about the market may not adequately reflect the company's actual circumstances.

### Reliance on Customer-Specific Assets or Processes

Some businesses make investments to meet the requirements of a particular customer. These may include specialised equipment, dedicated production capacity, customised software or customer-specific processes.

Such arrangements might be relevant if the customer relationship changes or ends. The potential effect will depend on whether the assets or capabilities can be redeployed, whether alternative demand exists and what costs may arise.

The existence of customer-specific investment does not, by itself, determine the value of the business. It is one factor that may need to be considered alongside the wider financial and commercial evidence.

## Customer Concentration and Financial Forecasts

Where forecasts form part of a business valuation, assumptions about major customer relationships may be particularly relevant.

Forecasts may assume that existing customers continue purchasing at similar levels, that contracts are renewed or that revenue from new customers offsets any reduction in existing business. The evidence supporting those assumptions may require examination.

### Testing Revenue Assumptions

An expert may consider whether projected revenue is consistent with historical trading, existing contracts, customer purchasing patterns and the business's commercial circumstances.

Where a forecast depends heavily on a small number of customers, the analysis may need to identify the assumptions concerning those relationships and consider whether the available evidence supports them.

This does not mean that a forecast based on concentrated revenue is necessarily unreliable. Its significance depends on the evidence and assumptions underlying the projection.

### Considering Alternative Scenarios

In some circumstances, considering alternative scenarios may help explain how different customer outcomes could affect projected financial performance.

For example, the analysis might consider the effect of a reduction in orders from a major customer, a delayed contract renewal or the acquisition of replacement business. Whether such scenarios are appropriate will depend on the valuation question and the evidence available.

Scenario analysis should not be confused with a prediction that a particular event will occur. It can instead illustrate the sensitivity of a valuation analysis to material assumptions.

## Customer Concentration and Valuation Methodology

Customer concentration does not automatically require a particular valuation method or adjustment. Its relevance depends on the valuation approach adopted and how the underlying commercial risk is reflected in the analysis.

### Income-Based Valuation

Where an income-based approach is used, customer concentration may affect the assessment of projected revenue, margins or future cash flows.

The expert may need to consider whether the forecasts adequately reflect the evidence concerning significant customer relationships. Care is also needed to avoid accounting for the same risk more than once through overlapping assumptions or adjustments.

### Market-Based Valuation

Where comparable companies or transactions are considered, differences in customer concentration may affect the comparability of the available evidence.

A business with a diversified customer base may have different commercial characteristics from one that relies heavily on a single customer. However, the relevance of that difference will depend on the circumstances of the businesses and the other factors affecting the comparison.

Any conclusion about comparability should therefore be supported by the available evidence rather than by customer concentration alone.

## Customer Concentration in Valuation Disputes

In litigation, customer concentration may become a point of disagreement where the parties place different emphasis on the stability or significance of particular commercial relationships.

One valuation analysis may treat a major customer relationship as a relatively stable source of revenue. Another may place greater emphasis on renewal uncertainty, customer bargaining power or the cost of replacing lost business.

The expert's task is to address the valuation questions within the scope of the instruction and explain the basis for the analysis. This may involve identifying the evidence relied upon, distinguishing established facts from assumptions and explaining the limitations of the available information.

Where the parties disagree about customer concentration, it may be useful to identify the precise source of disagreement. The issue could concern the accuracy of the revenue data, the interpretation of a contract, the likelihood of renewal or the effect of a potential change in customer demand. These are distinct questions and may require different evidence.

## Questions Solicitors May Consider When Reviewing Customer Concentration Evidence

When reviewing a business valuation involving significant customer concentration, solicitors may wish to consider the following questions.

### How Much Revenue Depends on Major Customers?

The analysis should make clear how customer concentration has been measured and the period covered by the financial information.

### How Secure Are the Relevant Customer Relationships?

Contractual arrangements, renewal terms, trading history and other commercial evidence may help explain the basis for assumptions about continuing revenue.

### Can the Business Replace a Major Customer?

The availability of alternative customers, the time required to secure replacement business and any associated costs may be relevant, depending on the circumstances.

### How Has Customer Concentration Been Reflected in the Valuation?

It may be useful to understand whether customer concentration affects projected financial performance, the selection of comparable evidence or another aspect of the valuation analysis. The treatment will depend on the approach adopted and should be explained in the context of the evidence.

## Conclusion

Customer concentration can be a relevant consideration when valuing a business for litigation, particularly where a significant proportion of revenue or earnings depends on a small number of commercial relationships.

Its significance cannot be determined solely by counting customers or calculating the percentage of revenue attributable to the largest account. Contractual terms, customer retention, profitability, alternative demand and the wider commercial context may all be relevant.

A valuation expert may therefore need to examine the available financial and commercial evidence and consider how customer concentration relates to the assumptions used in the valuation. The appropriate analysis will depend on the circumstances of the business, the valuation date and the questions the expert has been instructed to address.
